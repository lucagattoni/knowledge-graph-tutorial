# BM25

This entry answers what BM25 is, where it came from, and why it is still the baseline that newer retrieval methods are measured against. The full formula is in the first source.

## Definition

BM25 scores a document against a query by adding up, for each query term, a weight built from three parts: how often the term occurs in the document (with a ceiling, so repeats count less and less), how rare the term is in the collection, and the document's length against the average [1]. It is the best-known instance of the probabilistic relevance framework, which ranks by estimated probability of relevance [1]. Without relevance judgements its rarity part reduces to a form of inverse document frequency, so it resembles [tf-idf](./tf-idf.md) with a saturating frequency part [1]. Two parameters, k1 and b, are tuned; about 1.2 to 2 and 0.5 to 0.8 are "reasonably good in many circumstances" [1]. It matches exact terms only [2].

## How it changed

- **1994.** The function came out of the Okapi retrieval system. Robertson and Zaragoza trace the original to a SIGIR 1994 paper by Robertson and Walker [1]; Lin et al. cite the Okapi report for TREC-3, also 1994 [2]. See [The Web changes data and search](../history/the-web.md).
- **20160408.** Lucene 6.0.0 was released [4]; its change log says the default similarity "is changed to BM25Similarity" [3]. The 2009 survey had said Lucene did not implement BM25 [1]. See [Knowledge graphs at scale](../history/knowledge-graphs-at-scale.md).
- **2020.** Karpukhin et al. report a [dense retriever](./dense-retrieval.md) beating "a strong Lucene-BM25 system" by 9 to 19 points absolute on top-20 passage accuracy (first-party) [5]. See [Language models meet retrieval](../history/language-models-meet-retrieval.md).
- **20260729 (v3 20260731).** Wang and seven co-authors report, over 28 corpus sizes, that BM25 overtakes a file-system agent near 10 million corpus tokens and leads at every larger shared size; graph-based variants stay below it [6]. Still moving: [Agentic search over raw data](../trends/agentic-search.md).

## Where sources disagree

**Which 1994 paper.** Robertson and Zaragoza name the SIGIR paper [1], Lin et al. the TREC-3 report [2], and Sanderson and Croft print the year as "199?" [7].

**Has dense retrieval replaced it?** Stonebraker and Pavlo write that inverted-index engines "have been supplanted in recent years" by embedding search [8]. Lin et al. call BM25 the starting point of many text-ranking approaches [2], and the 2026 study [6] finds it a strong default. No source read settles this.
